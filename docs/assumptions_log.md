# Assumptions & Decision Log

Assumptions and decisions made during the analysis, with the reason and the expected impact if an
assumption turns out to be wrong. The last column shows the notebook where each one was made.

## Assumptions

| # | Assumption | Why | Impact if wrong | Notebook |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | The plant buys electricity at the day-ahead spot price (dynamic contract). | Load shifting only saves money with a spot-linked contract. | With a fixed-price contract, savings ≈ 0 → we also report the savings a company *could* unlock by switching. | 01 |
| A2 | Grid fees, taxes and levies are the same at all times of day. | Simplification; they are mostly fixed per kWh. | Savings are calculated on the wholesale part only — stated clearly in results. | 01 |
| A3 | The 2017 HIPE load pattern is representative of a typical week today. | Production processes change slowly; we need the *shape*, not the date. | Absolute € numbers may differ; the method stays valid. | 01 |
| A4 | Flexible candidates: SolderingOven and VacuumPump1 (about 60% of active machine energy, running only 12% and 20% of the week); HighTemperatureOven as a secondary candidate (highest power, 3.6 kW, but rarely used). Jobs are shifted only within the weekday day shift. | All machines run only on weekdays during the day shift and work in batches; no access to the plant's production planners. | Some batches may be tied to the process order (e.g. oven and vacuum pump running together); to be checked in notebook 02. | 01 |
| A5 | 2024 SPARK loads are replayed against Oct 2025 – Sep 2026 prices by matching the same ISO week, weekday and quarter-hour. | Keeps the weekly rhythm (shifts, weekends) and the seasons; no machine data exists for the price year. | Public holidays fall on different days in different years, so a few days are slightly off. Yearly energy stays within ~1% (2024 has 366 days). | 02 |
| A6 | Measurement gaps are filled with the machine's average load for the same weekday and time of day (whole-year average). | The SPARK logger was offline for all 11 TEC machines at the same times (1,704 quarter-hours, 4.9% of 2024; longest 26 Jan – 4 Feb). Counting gaps as zero would understate cost by ~5%. | Filled periods follow the typical pattern, not the real one; the filled share is reported in the notebook. | 02 |
| A7 | Price forecasts use only price history and the calendar (no weather data). | Historical weather forecasts for 2–7 days ahead are hard to obtain for free; actual weather would leak future information. | Wind and solar drive the price level, so forecasts miss weather-driven days (e.g. a cloudy week without the usual midday dip). Weather features are the main improvement for later. | 03 |
| A8 | Coolers and chillers (cycling machines) follow the heat load of the workshop, so their production energy moves with the shift. | They cool the machines and the hall; their daytime load rises and falls with production. | If they also depend on outdoor temperature, part of their load would not move; Lever 2 would be slightly smaller. | 04 |
| A9 | Standby can be switched off on weekdays outside 03:00–17:00 and all weekend, with 1 h warm-up before the shift (window moves with the shift). | The data shows no production at those times; 1 h warm-up is a common rule of thumb. | Some CNC machines are kept warm for dimensional accuracy, or a cooler may serve another process. Must be confirmed with the operators before acting. | 04 |
| A10 | CO₂ per kWh = generation-weighted average of German power plants in each quarter-hour, with life-cycle emission factors (IPCC AR5 medians; typical values for lignite and other fuels). Imports and pumped storage are left out. | Simple, transparent and reproducible from public data. | Average factors understate the effect of shifting compared with marginal factors (the plant that actually reacts). Results are good for comparing schedules, not for an official carbon footprint. | 05 |

## Decisions

| # | Decision | Alternatives considered | Reason | Notebook |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Use 12 months of prices from 1 Oct 2025 – 30 Sep 2026. | Longer history incl. hourly prices. | Only the 15-minute market is relevant for today's decisions; 12 months covers all seasons. | 01 |
| D2 | Resample machine data from ~5 s to 15 min (mean kW). | Keep 5 s data. | Must match price resolution; 15-min mean power × 0.25 h = energy in kWh. | 01 |
| D3 | Energy-Charts API instead of manual SMARD download. | SMARD CSV export. | Reproducible: anyone can re-run the script. Same underlying data. | 01 |
| D4 | Keep extreme prices (min −499.99 €/MWh on 1 May 2026 13:15–14:15; max 747.10 €/MWh on 24 Jun 2026 20:45, 700+ €/MWh on 14 Sep 2026 19:15–20:00). | Remove or cap outliers. | Validated as real market events: price floor at midday on a public holiday (solar surplus, low demand), several consecutive slots; maxima in the evening when solar drops. Extremes are where load shifting saves money, so removing them would understate savings. Use median alongside mean. | 01 |
| D5 | Treat small negative power values (≥ −0.03 kW) as sensor noise and set them to 0. | Keep as-is; treat as energy feed-back. | They occur only when machines are off and are tiny; none of these machines can generate electricity. | 01 |
| D6 | Exclude ChipPress, WashingMachine and VacuumOven from the flexibility analysis for this week. | Keep all 10 machines. | Average ≈ 0.00 kW, so they were idle or in standby during 23–29 Oct 2017. | 01 |
| D7 | Use HIPE to build and test the method; add KIT's SPARK dataset (22 heavier machines plus PV) for the main business case. | Rely on HIPE only. | The 10 metered HIPE machines use only ~102 kWh/week, about 4% of the plant's 2,714 kWh/week, so the business case would be only a few hundred € per year. | 01 |
| D8 | Use the 11 TEC workshop machines (mills, lathes, wire EDM, chillers) from SPARK for 2024, active power `P_total` only. | All 22 SPARK machines and all years; other measurements. | The TEC machines are the heavy, realistic production machines and are only recorded in 2024; one measurement per machine keeps the download small. | 02 |
| D9 | Report an upper bound before optimising: each day's production energy placed in the cheapest quarter-hours of the same day (shift window 06:00–22:00 first), capped at the machine's peak power. | Go straight to optimisation. | Shows whether the business case can be worth it at all; if the ceiling is small, stop early. Realistic savings (notebook 04) will be lower. | 02 |
| D10 | Split each machine's energy into standby (median power when the workshop is closed: weekends and 00:00–05:00) and production (everything above). Only production energy counts as shiftable. | Treat all energy as shiftable. | Standby cannot be moved, only switched off. On real data it is 48% of all energy (DNG 50 evo: 4.1 kW day and night). Counting it as shiftable inflated the upper bound from €2.2k to €3.8k. | 02 |
| D11 | Forecast setup: the plan is made on day D, prices up to D+1 are known (noon auction), days D+2 … D+7 are forecast. Every feature must be known on day D. | Forecast 1 day ahead; use any available data. | Matches how a weekly production plan is made; prevents data leakage. | 03 |
| D12 | Rolling-origin backtest: test months Mar–Sep 2026, each trained only on days up to 7 days before the month. | One random train/test split. | A random split leaks future information into training; the monthly roll imitates real use. | 03 |
| D13 | Use the 7-day average forecast for planning in notebook 04; LightGBM tested and not used. | LightGBM. | The 7-day average has the lowest error overall (MAE 37.9 vs 38.8 €/MWh), the best within-day ranking, and captures 96–98% of the perfect-knowledge saving vs. 92–93% for LightGBM. Simplest method that works. | 03 |
| D14 | Classify machines by median production-run length: > 24 h continuous, 2–24 h shift-bound, < 2 h cycling. | Treat all energy as freely movable (upper bound from notebook 02). | Real runs are not small movable jobs: the wire EDM runs for days, mills run one block per shift. Only whole shifts can move. | 04 |
| D15 | Lever 2 moves the whole weekday shift by whole hours (0 to +4 h later, never earlier than today); the continuous wire EDM stays fixed. | Move single jobs; allow earlier starts. | Matches how a workshop can actually change its schedule; the shift already starts at 04:00–05:00, earlier is not realistic for staff. | 04 |
| D16 | Recommend standby off + a fixed summer schedule (Apr–Sep, +4 h). The weekly forecast plan is documented as an alternative. | Weekly forecast-based shift planning. | The summer schedule gets 92% of the weekly plan's saving (€337 vs €365) with one fixed rule instead of changing working hours every week. | 04 |
| D17 | Use enumeration of all options instead of linear programming. | Linear programming (MILP). | The decision space is tiny (5 shift options per week), so trying every option is exact, fast and easier to explain. LP would add complexity without changing the answer. | 04 |
| D18 | One results module (`src/results.py`) writes small CSV tables that feed the notebook, the Streamlit app and Power BI. | Calculate numbers separately in each tool. | All three always show the same numbers; the dashboards stay fast and need no raw data (which is not in Git). | 05 |
| D19 | Keep +4 h as the calculated plan, but advise the workshop to start with +2 h. | Switch the plan to the combined optimum (+3 h). | Checked both levers together: the saving is almost flat between +2 h and +4 h (€3,279–3,313), so +2 h gets 99% of it with a much smaller change for the workers. | 06 |
